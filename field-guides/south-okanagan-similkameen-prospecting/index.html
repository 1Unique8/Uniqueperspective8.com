Tracing alluvial float

South Okanagan and Similkameen ground. A working method from gravel bar to gossan — then a number on the stone before it leaves the dirt.

Geological setting of the Southern Interior

The South Okanagan and Similkameen offer a landscape of Eocene volcanic flows and hydrothermal vein systems. Glacial moraines and living river drainages redistribute mineral wealth across the valley floors. The stone in your hand may have traveled — or it may still be pointing uphill. Which of those two stories does its shape tell you first?

Key collection zones

  • Similkameen River. Extensive gravel bars that act as natural concentrators for heavy minerals.
  • Okanagan River corridor. Especially around Oliver, with diverse mineral occurrences.
  • Hedley. Historic mineralized skarns and a hard-rock mining legacy.
  • Tulameen system. A primary study target for the Tulameen placer suite.

Law first. Read Guide 01 — Free Miner & field code before you walk a bar or a cut. Syilx territory and active claims are not scenery.

The science of float tracing

Transport writes on the specimen. River action produces rounded, smooth stones. Angular talus or scree has not traveled far from its origin. Distinguishing the two is the first step from alluvium to outcrop.

Mapping a float train

  1. Identify the train. Locate a concentration of mineralized float in a drainage.
  2. Move upstream / uphill. Follow the float toward higher ground.
  3. Watch physical change. Size and angularity should increase as you close on the source.
  4. Watch mineralization. Sulfide expression often strengthens nearer the structure.

If size and angularity do not increase as you climb, what other story might the drainage be telling — a glacial dump, a second source, or a cutoff?

Bedrock traps between float and outcrop

  • Bedrock crevices and cracks
  • Gravel bars on the inside bends of rivers
  • Plunge pools at the base of waterfalls
  • Confluences where gullies meet the main drainage

The gossan guideline

If it is rusty and rotten, gold is not forgotten — but rust is not a certificate. Gossan is the oxidized cap of a vein where sulfides have weathered. Texture first, color second. The longer list lives in Guide 04.

  1. Cellular boxwork. Delicate lattice voids left when sulfides leach out of quartz.
  2. Oxidation halos. Brick-red hematite or ochre-yellow limonite staining.
  3. Vuggy and fractured quartz. Pitted, sugary, or brecciated quartz is more likely to host minerals than solid milky bull quartz.
  4. Contact selvages. Sheared or altered boundaries between vein and wall-rock.
  5. Heavy accessory minerals. Concentrated iron and manganese oxides inside the vein body.

Field toolkit

  • 22 oz Estwing geological hammer. Breaking and prying.
  • Cold chisels. Narrow-vein sampling.
  • 10x achromatic triplet hand lens. Crystals and boxwork.
  • Neodymium magnet. Magnetite and pyrrhotite.
  • Spray bottle. Wet stone reveals color and texture the dry surface hides.

Safety

  • PPE. Eye protection and heavy leather gloves when striking rock.
  • Wildlife. Bear spray and attention. This is interior country.
  • Terrain. Steep talus and fast water are not optional hazards.
  • Communication. A check-in protocol before remote ground.

Field logging before the stone leaves

The value of a specimen is tied to its data. Logging begins at the site, not at the bench. If you cannot reconstruct the site from the card, what have you actually collected?

  • Date
  • Precise GPS coordinates
  • Access notes — how the site was reached
  • Host rock and geological context
  • Initial observations: physical character and mineral associations

Assign a permanent field ID in the UP8-### format before any stone is removed. That number is the thread from dirt to cabinet. See Guide 05.
