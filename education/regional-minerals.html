South Okanagan & Similkameen stones

Volcanic ash, old riverbeds, and metamorphic heat. The region writes in iron, silica, and contact textures.

Regional species profiles

The geological diversity of the South Okanagan and Similkameen offers specimen-grade material if you test before you name.

Red & polychrome jasper

Microcrystalline silica colored primarily by iron oxides. Conchoidal (shell-like) fracture. Mohs hardness 6.5–7.0. Polychrome varieties show swirling red, brown, and tan. Look for a river-tumbled skin and a body that holds up under the saw.

Okanagan golden & yellow jasper

Distinct from the red varieties. Color comes from limonite infusions inside a cryptocrystalline quartz matrix. Warm, earthy saturation. Often found near older workings in the Oliver / Hedley country.

Chalcedony & agate

Fibrous quartz with waxy luster and translucent edges. Agates are the banded members of the family, formed by rhythmic deposition in volcanic or sedimentary cavities.

Black chert, argillite & siltstone

Fine-grained sedimentary and metasedimentary rocks, often dark from organic carbon. Generally opaque. They lack the waxy luster of true chalcedony — that difference is visible under edge-lighting.

Optical calcite / Iceland spar

Rhombohedral cleavage. Double refraction: objects viewed through a clear piece appear doubled. Mohs hardness 3.0. Reacts vigorously to the acid fizz test. Do not confuse softness and fizz with quartz.

Galena & sulfide ores

Historical mining districts in the region still yield galena: brilliant metallic luster, high density (heavy for its size), and perfect cubic cleavage that breaks into 90-degree steps. Treat sulfides as specimens first, ore second.

Altered skarns, argillite, and schist

Contact stories: hot magmatic fluids meeting sedimentary rock. Dense assemblages and textures that do not look like river jasper at all.
