Guide 02 · Tests, not guesses

Field identification & physical testing

Color lies. Repeatable tests do not. Five diagnostics classify most common minerals without a lab. Carry a small kit and write what the stone actually does.

The 5-step physical diagnostic protocol

Effective field identification is a sequence, not a vibe. Run the same tests in the same order so notes from two days can still talk to each other.

Step 1: Mohs scratch hardness

The Mohs scale measures resistance to being scratched. In the field, common objects approximate hardness:

  • Fingernail (~2.5) — scratches soft minerals like gypsum or talc.
  • Copper penny (~3.5) — scratches calcite but not fluorite.
  • Pocket-knife steel blade (~5.5) — scratches glass and some silicates.
  • Unglazed glass plate (~6.5) — used to test harder minerals.

Microcrystalline quartz varieties — jasper, chert, and chalcedony — typically test between 6.5–7.0. They scratch glass easily and resist a steel knife.

Step 2: Streak testing

Streak is the color of a mineral in powdered form. It is often more consistent than the color of the external specimen. Drag the mineral across an unglazed porcelain tile:

  • Hematite — reddish-brown streak.
  • Pyrite — greenish-black streak.
  • Native gold — distinctive golden-yellow streak.

Step 3: LED translucency & edge-lighting

Hold a high-output white LED against the stone or inspect thin margins:

  • Translucent — light passes through the edges. Hallmark of chalcedony and agate.
  • Opaque — light is blocked. Typical of siltstone, argillite, or dense chert.

Step 4: Longwave UV fluorescence (365 nm)

A 365 nm UV light can reveal secondary mineral deposition. Calcite veining often fluoresces white, yellow, or red. Inert silica typically does not react.

Step 5: Dilute acid reaction (fizz test)

A drop of dilute hydrochloric acid — or household vinegar, more slowly — tests for calcium carbonate.

  • Carbonates (calcite / limestone) — vigorous fizz.
  • Silicates — remain inert.

Handle acid as a field chemical, not a toy. A drop is enough. Neutralize and pack the bottle out. Never test a finished jewelry face.

Field lapidary potential

Before committing a specimen to the saw or tumbler, look once more:

  1. Micro-fractures. Internal checks can shatter the stone during cutting.
  2. Structural cohesion. Avoid crumbly or vuggy material unless that texture is the point.
  3. Softness gradients. In multi-mineral stones, a soft zone polishes away while the hard zone stays proud.
  4. Surface rind. Measure how much weathered skin must come off before you reach the heart.
