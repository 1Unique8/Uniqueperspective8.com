What we mean by jasper here

Jasper, as used in this hub, is opaque to barely translucent microcrystalline quartz colored by iron oxides. In Similkameen country that often reads as red, brick, ochre, tan, or polychrome swirl. Fracture is conchoidal. The name is a regional field name for material found as float or in lawful public cuts — not a mine brand.

How it should test

Run the same five steps as the identification guide. Predict first: if this is jasper, what must be true?

Mohs hardnessScratches glass. Resists a knife.Soft or crumbly: siltstone, weathered volcanic, or carbonate with iron paint.
StreakOften pale. Hematite-rich zones can smear reddish-brown.A bold red-brown streak with low hardness points to hematite pigment, not a silica body.
LED edge-lightMostly opaque. A thin sliver may glow dull red, not watery-clear.Bright watery margins suggest chalcedony or agate. See the agate guide.

AcidNo fizz on a fresh silica face.Fizz means carbonate. Iron stain on limestone is not jasper.

What it is not

  • Not every red river rock. Iron travels farther than silica does.
  • Not agate unless banding and translucency are actually there.
  • Not chert by default. Compare on the regional page.
  • Not “from the mine.” Studio cards do not borrow a camp name for a cobble picked on public ground.

Provenance and lawful ground

If a Similkameen jasper leaves this house with a card, the card may carry a river system or public cut, a date, a collector, and FMC 292907 when that standing applied. Read Provenance and the BC Free Miner Stewardship Standard.
