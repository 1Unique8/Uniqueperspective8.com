Dug by hand. Crafted by heart. Fall 7, stand 8. A written account of how Unique Perspective 8 collects, verifies, and documents stone before it reaches a bench or a cabinet.

Why provenance has to be visible

The global mineral trade often hides the path from ground to glass case. Untreated and treated stone travel in the same boxes. Dyes, synthetics, and undisclosed enhancements make a river cobble look like something it is not, and they make the environmental and social cost of extraction harder to see.

The studio answer is vertical: the same hands that check title, walk the corridor, log the find, and polish the piece also write the card that travels with it. If a sentence cannot be tied to a date, a place, and a method, it does not belong on the listing.

Recreational collecting versus commercial standing

All extraction in the province sits under the British Columbia Mineral Tenure Act. The first question is not “what did I find?” It is “which permission do I actually hold?”

  • Recreational rockhounding is small-scale personal collecting. It is not a license to sell, not a claim, and not an excuse for mechanized disturbance.
  • The BC Free Miner Certificate (FMC) is the standing required for small-scale commercial collecting and lawful prospecting. It makes the acquisition of material legally transparent. It is not a mine permit.
  • Hand tools only. Hammers, chisels, and pans. No motors. No heavy excavation. Stewardship starts with the method, not the slogan.

Morgan holds an FMC. That number belongs on a provenance card when the stone was taken under that standing. It does not travel onto every object in the shop. Read the shorter field code in Guide 01 and the bench claims on Ethics.

Verifying land before the first hole

Due diligence happens on Mineral Titles Online (MTO) before fieldwork begins. The registry is how we stay inside legal boundaries.

  • Identify unencumbered Crown land.
  • Check for active mineral claims or placer claims held by other parties.
  • Distinguish public land, private property, and protected parklands.
  • Respect existing tenure holders by reading the boundary instead of guessing it.

“It looked empty” is not a title search.

Territorial respect and cultural heritage

This work takes place on the unceded territory of the Syilx Okanagan Nation across the South Okanagan and Similkameen valleys. Stewardship is not only bank stability. It is respect for the cultural history of the land.

In compliance with the British Columbia Heritage Conservation Act, archaeological artifacts, historic structures, rock art, pictographs, and fossil sites stay where they are. The protocol for an unexpected cultural discovery is absolute:

  • Leave the site untouched.
  • Record precise GPS coordinates.
  • Notify provincial authorities immediately.

Riparian protection

The health of the Similkameen and Okanagan river systems comes before a specimen. Field work prioritizes riverbank and riparian conservation so fish habitat, bank stability, and native vegetation remain intact.

Leave No Trace, as practiced here:

  • Pack it in, pack it out. No waste left behind.
  • Backfill. Every test pit is closed to the natural contour of the land.
  • Minimal footprint. Presence should leave no lasting visual or physical scar.

Field log to finished jewelry

Every significant find receives a permanent identifier at the moment it leaves the ground — for example UP8-050-SIM. The number is the spine of the record, not a marketing code added later.

A physical provenance card travels with the order. It certifies:

  • Date and location of the find
  • Geological formation
  • Free Miner Certificate registration number, when that standing applied
  • Artisan signature

Studio mounting is solid 925 dead-soft recycled sterling silver. No synthetic resins. No artificial coatings that hide the stone. Drop-shipped goods sit outside these sentences and must be labeled as such. The paper trail is spelled out in Guide 05.
